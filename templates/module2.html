Title 22 for RCFE Administrators

Regulations are not just rules to memorize. An effective administrator must know how to identify the issue, gather the facts, locate the correct authority, apply it correctly, and document what happened.

6 Lessons • Self-Paced Training
Lesson 1 of 6

Title 22 Is a Decision-Making Tool

Administrators do not need to memorize every regulation word-for-word. They do need to know how to use regulations when making operational, safety, resident-care, and compliance decisions.

Use this five-step process:
  1. Identify the issue.
  2. Gather the facts.
  3. Locate the applicable authority.
  4. Apply the requirement to the facts.
  5. Document the decision and follow-up.

A strong administrator does not begin with, “What do I think happened?” The administrator begins with, “What do I know, what do I need to verify, and what requirement applies?”

Lesson 2 of 6

Facts Before Conclusions

One of the most important administrator skills is separating facts from assumptions, opinions, and conclusions.

Example:

A family member reports that their mother's call button was ignored “for almost an hour.”

The statement is important, but it is not yet a verified timeline.

What should the administrator review?

  • Call-system records
  • Staff assignment sheets
  • Caregiver statements
  • Resident interview, when appropriate
  • Progress notes
  • Care plan information
  • Other objective documentation
Key principle: Preserve the allegation exactly as reported, then investigate before reaching a conclusion.
Lesson 3 of 6

Read the Regulation Carefully

Regulatory language matters. Small words can change the meaning of an entire requirement.

Watch for words such as:

  • Shall
  • Must
  • May
  • Unless
  • Except
  • Immediately
  • Within
A staff member tells the administrator:

“Licensing told me years ago that we were allowed to do it this way.”

What should the administrator do?
Best response:
Verify the current regulation, statute, licensing guidance, or other applicable authority rather than relying on memory or an old verbal interpretation.
Lesson 4 of 6

Resident Rights and Safety

Administrators frequently have to balance resident rights, family requests, safety concerns, and regulatory requirements.

A family tells staff:

“Do not let our mother leave with anyone unless we personally approve it.”

The administrator should not automatically implement the family's request simply because the family believes it is safest.

The administrator should determine:

  • What the resident wants
  • The resident's legal decision-making status
  • Whether an authorized representative is involved
  • Whether there is an immediate safety concern
  • What restrictions are legally permissible
  • What applicable resident-rights requirements say
Safety concerns matter, but safety does not automatically erase resident rights.
Lesson 5 of 6

Defensible Documentation

Documentation should allow another qualified person to understand what happened without needing the writer to explain it later.

Strong documentation identifies:

  • Date and time
  • Who was involved
  • What was directly observed
  • What was reported and by whom
  • Actions taken
  • Who was notified
  • Resident response or outcome
  • Required follow-up
Avoid: changing wording simply to make an event “sound better.” Documentation should be accurate, objective, complete, and defensible.
