LESSON 1

Title 22 Is a Decision-Making Tool

Administrators sometimes treat Title 22 as a giant list of rules that must be memorized. That approach creates unnecessary anxiety and often leads to poor decisions.

Your job is not to memorize every regulation. Your job is to recognize when a situation raises a regulatory issue and know how to find, interpret, apply, and document the applicable requirement.

A strong administrator asks: What is the actual issue, what are the facts, what authority applies, and what action best protects the resident?

The five-step regulatory process

  1. Identify the issue.
  2. Gather the facts.
  3. Verify the authority.
  4. Apply the requirement to the facts.
  5. Document the decision and follow-up.
Example:

A caregiver tells you a resident's daughter has instructed staff not to allow her mother to leave the building with a longtime friend.

Before simply following the daughter's instruction, an administrator must determine the resident's rights, decision-making authority, safety considerations, physician information, responsible-party documentation, and whether any legal authority exists to restrict the resident.

LESSON 2

Start With Facts — Not Conclusions

Licensing problems often grow because someone reaches a conclusion before determining what actually happened.

Consider the difference between these two statements:

Conclusion:
“The caregiver neglected the resident.”
Facts:
“The resident activated the call system at approximately 2:10 PM. Staff entered the room at approximately 2:18 PM. The resident reported needing assistance to the restroom.”

Regulations should be applied to verified facts whenever possible. Interviews, records, care plans, medication records, incident reports, staffing assignments, observation, video if legally available, and other documentation may all help establish what occurred.

Before deciding what regulation applies, ask:

  • What exactly happened?
  • When did it happen?
  • Who was present?
  • What did the resident need?
  • What did the care plan require?
  • What did staff actually do?
  • What documentation exists?
  • Was anyone notified?
  • Was the resident harmed or placed at risk?
LESSON 3

Read the Regulation Carefully

A common compliance mistake is relying on what someone remembers Title 22 says rather than reading the actual requirement.

When reviewing a regulation, pay attention to words such as:

  • shall
  • must
  • may
  • unless
  • except
  • immediately
  • within a specified period
One word can change whether a requirement is mandatory, permitted, conditional, or subject to an exception.

Read beyond one sentence.

A subsection may not make sense by itself. Review the surrounding section, definitions, exceptions, resident-rights provisions, and any related requirements before reaching a conclusion.

LESSON 4

Resident Rights Must Stay in the Analysis

Safety concerns do not automatically erase resident rights. Administrators frequently have to balance protection, autonomy, choice, dignity, privacy, and regulatory responsibility.

A rule created for staff convenience is not automatically a lawful restriction on a resident.

Case study: Family instruction

A resident's adult daughter tells the administrator: “My mother is not allowed to go outside anymore. Tell your staff she cannot leave.”

The resident objects and says she wants to continue sitting in the courtyard every afternoon.

What is the best first administrative response?

The correct question is rarely: “What does the family want?” or “What is easiest for staff?”

The better question is: “What is legally authorized, clinically appropriate, consistent with the resident's rights, and necessary for safe care?”
LESSON 5

Documentation Makes Your Decision Defensible

Even a reasonable decision becomes difficult to defend if nobody can determine why it was made.

Your documentation should show the reasoning process, not simply the final conclusion.

A defensible administrative note may include:

  • The issue identified.
  • Relevant facts gathered.
  • People interviewed or consulted.
  • Records reviewed.
  • Applicable policy or regulatory requirement.
  • Resident-specific considerations.
  • Action taken.
  • Notifications completed.
  • Follow-up or monitoring plan.
Avoid documentation designed to make an event “sound better.” Documentation should be factual, objective, accurate, timely, and complete.

Licensing arrives six weeks later.

Ask yourself: Could another qualified administrator read your documentation and understand what happened, what you considered, and why you made the decision?

If the answer is yes, your documentation is far more defensible.

The Administrator Standard

Issue → Facts → Authority → Application → Documentation

Use this sequence whenever you face a difficult compliance question. It prevents decisions based solely on rumor, habit, fear of licensing, or staff preference.
