Module 2: Read It. Interpret It. Apply It.

Using Title 22 as an RCFE Administrator

Regulations are not simply rules to memorize. Effective administrators must know how to identify an issue, gather facts, locate the correct authority, apply the requirement, and document their response.

6 Lessons • Self-Paced RCFE Administrator Training
Lesson 1 of 6

Title 22 Is a Decision-Making Tool

Administrators do not need to memorize every regulation word-for-word. They do need to know how to locate and correctly apply regulations when making operational, safety, resident-care, and compliance decisions.

Use this five-step process:
  1. Identify the issue.
  2. Gather the facts.
  3. Locate the applicable authority.
  4. Apply the requirement to the facts.
  5. Document the decision and follow-up.

A strong administrator does not begin with, "What do I think happened?"

The administrator begins with: "What do I know, what still needs to be verified, and what requirement applies?"

Lesson 2 of 6

Facts Before Conclusions

One of the most important administrator skills is separating facts from assumptions, opinions, allegations, and conclusions.

Scenario:

A family member reports that their mother's call button was ignored "for almost an hour."

The statement is important and must be taken seriously, but it is not yet a verified timeline.

What should the administrator review?

  • Call-system records
  • Staff assignment sheets
  • Caregiver statements
  • Resident interview, when appropriate
  • Progress notes
  • Care-plan information
  • Other objective documentation
Key Principle:
Preserve the allegation exactly as reported, then investigate before reaching a conclusion.
Lesson 3 of 6

Read the Regulation Carefully

Regulatory language matters. A single word can change the meaning of an entire requirement.

Watch carefully for words such as:

  • Shall
  • Must
  • May
  • Unless
  • Except
  • Immediately
  • Within
Scenario:

A staff member tells the administrator:

"Licensing told me years ago that we were allowed to do it this way."

What should the administrator do?

Best Response:

Verify the current regulation, statute, licensing guidance, or other applicable authority rather than relying on memory or an old verbal interpretation.

Lesson 4 of 6

Resident Rights and Safety

Administrators frequently have to balance resident rights, family requests, safety concerns, and regulatory requirements.

Scenario:

A family tells staff:

"Do not let our mother leave with anyone unless we personally approve it."

The administrator should not automatically implement the family's request simply because the family believes it is safest.

The administrator should determine:

  • What the resident wants
  • The resident's legal decision-making status
  • Whether an authorized representative is involved
  • Whether there is an immediate safety concern
  • What restrictions are legally permissible
  • What applicable resident-rights requirements say
Remember:

Safety concerns matter, but safety does not automatically erase resident rights.

Lesson 5 of 6

Defensible Documentation

Documentation should allow another qualified person to understand what happened without needing the writer to explain the event later.

Strong documentation identifies:

  • Date and time
  • Who was involved
  • What was directly observed
  • What was reported and by whom
  • Actions taken
  • Who was notified
  • Resident response or outcome
  • Required follow-up
Avoid:

Changing wording simply to make an event "sound better." Documentation should be accurate, objective, complete, and defensible.

Lesson 6 of 6

Complaints and Conflicting Statements

Complaints often involve multiple people who remember the same event differently. The administrator's job is not to immediately decide who is telling the truth.

Scenario:

A resident reports that a caregiver refused to help her.

The caregiver states that assistance was offered, but the resident declined.

Another staff member reports hearing an argument but did not see how the interaction began.

What should the administrator do?

  • Document each person's statement accurately.
  • Identify what can be independently verified.
  • Review relevant records and care-plan information.
  • Determine whether immediate protective action is necessary.
  • Apply applicable requirements to the verified facts.
  • Document findings, actions, notifications, and follow-up.
Best Response:

Preserve the facts first. Do not rewrite conflicting statements into one convenient version of the event.
